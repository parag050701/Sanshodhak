Abstract: Medical image retrieval technologies are fundamental in modern healthcare since they allow doctors to rapidly 
obtain relevant past cases and support accurate diagnosis. This paper offers a new hybrid feature extracting method 
designed to increase the efficiency and effectiveness of content-based medical image retrieval (CBMIR). By using local 
and global feature descriptors, the model provides a full and discriminative representation of medical images. Combining 
texture-based global features including Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) and Local Binary Patterns (LBP) with 
local features such Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) and Speeded-Up Robust Features (SURF provides strong 
picture characterisation. To maximise computing speed, dimensionality is reduced using Principal Component Analysis 
(PCA); Support Vector Machines (SVMs) are utilised for classification thus making use of their capacity in managing 
high-dimensional, non-linear data. Experimental evaluation on many medical imaging datasets including MRI, CT, and 
X-ray modalities shows ret retrieval accuracy, reaction speed, and scalability, thereby underlining the benefits of the 
approach. With an average query response time of 0.8 seconds and a retrieval accuracy of 93.5%, the hybrid approach 
outperforms traditional single-feature-based systems. Providing a significant contribution to clinical workflows, research, 
and instructional uses, this development addresses critical issues in medical picture handling including variability across 
modalities and scalability to large datasets. The outcomes show the opportunities of hybrid feature extraction and machine 
learning integration to alter medical image retrieval systems, therefore paving the door for further advances employing 
domain-specific adaptations and deep learning techniques.

Background and Importance 
Since medical imaging gives doctors complete 
knowledge of a patient's anatomy and pathology, modern 
healthcare depends significantly on it. Essential for 
diagnosis, treatment planning, and longitudinal studies, 
modalities including X-rays, MRI, CT scans, and 
ultrasounds provide daily large amounts of data [2]. 
These images offer relevance for research, education, 
and case-based analysis even outside of direct 
therapeutic use. These advances however still provide a 
significant bottleneck in obtaining relevant images 
swiftly and correctly. Usually dependent on manually 
submitted metadata, conventional retrieval methods rely 
largely on text-based search engines. This approach is 
not only time-consuming but also prone to errors and 
inconsistencies especially in circumstances when 
metadata does not adequately reflect the image content. 
Content-Based Medical Image Retrieval (CBMIR) has 
become a possible method stressing internal image 
properties including shape, texture, and patterns. Still, 
present systems can find it difficult to handle the 
diversity and complexity of medical images [3]. This 
work stresses the relevance of CBMIR and proposes a 
hybrid feature extraction framework employing global 
and local elements to increase retrieval performance in 
order to satisfy the growing demand for powerful and 
efficient medical image management systems. 
 
Challenges in Medical Image Retrieval 
Medical image retrieval offers various challenges 
largely linked to the natural complexity and variability 
of medical imaging data. Medical images vary widely 
among modalities, including MRI, CT, and ultrasonic 
waves, each with unique characteristics that give 
particular 
challenge 
for 
feature 
extraction 
and 
representation. Furthermore, adding still another degree 
of difficulty are pathogenic aberrations within the same 
modality. Development of one-size-fits-all feature 
extraction models is difficult, for example, since 
tumours in CT images can differ greatly in size, form, 
and texture. Conventional CBMIR systems based on 
single-feature descriptors can find it challenging to 
generalise across several variants, which generates less 
than optimal retrieval performance. Moreover, the great 
amount of medical imaging data in modern hospitals 
causes scaling issues. Systems have to efficiently 
manage tens of thousands to millions of images without 
compromising accuracy or retrieval speed in design [4]. 
Still another great challenge are interpretability and 
clinical relevance. Retracted images must not only 
match the search but also provide useful information to 
back up clinical conclusions. This work addresses these 
challenges by suggesting a hybrid feature extraction 
method combining local and global aspects, optimised 
for classification and retrieval using Support Vector 
Machines (SVMs). By means of solving these issues, the 
suggested method aims to significantly increase the 
accuracy and efficiency of medical picture retrieval.

Support Vector Machines and their relevance in 
machine learning 
Strong tools in machine learning with great appeal for 
their ability to shine in both classification and regression 
issues are Support Vector machines (SVMs). Basically, 
searching the hyperplane splitting data points into 
multiple classes with the best margin of error, support 
vector machines (SVMs) operate. The margin is every 
class's nearest data point's distance from the hyperplane. 
This margin shows support vectors. Support vector 
machines (SVM) maximise this margin so that they may 
generate a decision boundary lowering the overfitting 
risk and increasing the generalising capability of the 
model [12]. The program's key benefit is supporting 
vector machines' (SVM) capacity to address both linear 
and non-linear classification challenges. In cases when 
the data can be split linearly, a support vector machine 
(SVM) can rapidly find the ideal hyperplane for class 
splitting. Practically, then, data is not always linearly 
separable. 
The kernel method addresses this problem for the 
support vector machine (SVM). This technique 
emphasises 
data 
transformation 
into 
a 
higher-
dimensional space enabling linear separation. By use of 
popular kernel functions such sigmoid, polyn, and radial 
basis functions (RBFs), support vector machines 
(SVMs) replicate complex decision boundaries and find 
detailed patterns in data. Support vector machines 
(SVMs) are the foundation of machine learning for 
reasons not just linked to their vast applicability in many 
different disciplines. Applications including sentiment 
analysis and spam detection use statistical vector 
machines (SVMs), for text categorising [13]. These 
operations depend on their precise manipulation of high-
dimensional feature spaces. Especially in front of 
difficult visual input, support vector machines' (SVMs') 
capacity to properly classify images and identify objects 
adds a great value to computer vision. In bioinformatics, 
the application of support vector machines (SVMs) for 
purposes such gene classification and protein structure 
prediction increase further evidence of their versatility 
and strength. 
Support 
vector 
machines 
(SVMs) 
have 
some 
disadvantages even if they offer many benefits. Two 
such include the high computational costs of large 
datasets and the necessity of exact hyperparameter 
tuning. But as computer resources and optimising 
techniques advance, support vector machines (SVMs) 
are growingly scalable and efficient. All things 
considered, for practitioners in machine learning SVMs 
are a flexible and essential tool [14]. For many different 
kinds of classification and regression projects, they 
produce consistent answers. Their versatility, efficiency 
across a wide range of applications, and ability to 
manage both linear and non-linear challenges clearly 
illustrate their importance and ongoing relevance in the 
field of machine learning.

Research Methodology 
 
Fig Research Methodology 
 
Design and development of the SVM-based image 
retrieval model follows a three-fold research process 
including numerous important phases. To guarantee the 
generalizability of the model, first a big and varied 
picture 
dataset 
is 
chosen. 
Resizing, 
grayscale 
conversion, noise reduction—all of which help to 
normalize the images—are preprocessing techniques. 
Following preprocessing, the model moves on feature 
extraction, in which case essential elements including 
color, texture, and form capture the visual content of 
every picture. Using color histograms and color 
moments, color characteristics are retrieved; texture 
features are recovered using Local Binary Patterns 
(LBP) and Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM); 
shape features are extracted using Canny Edge Detector. 
The feature vectors—which represent every picture in a 
multi-dimensional space—are built after the feature 
extraction is finished. Sometimes dimensionality 
reduction methods like as PCA are used to lower 
computing complexity and raise model efficiency. The 
Support Vector Machine (SVM) is then trained on these 
feature vectors using a half of the dataset for training and 
the other half for testing. To guarantee correct 
classification, the SVM searches for the ideal hyperplane 
maximizing the margin between many picture classes. 
To 
maximize 
performance, 
the 
SVM's 
hyperparameters—including the kind of kernel and 
regularizing parameter—are tweaked. At last, the 
model's retrieval accuracy and efficiency are assessed by 
means of comprehensive testing employing unseen 
query photos. 
The study approach is meant to methodically solve the 
picture retrieval issue using Support Vector Machines 
(SVMs). It describes the methodical process followed in 
development, evaluation, and optimization of the 
suggested image retrieval system. Data collecting, 
preprocessing, feature extraction, SVM implementation, 
model training, and validation are discussed in this part.

The high testing accuracy implies that the SVM 
classifier did not overfit the training data. Overfitting 
would occur if the model performed significantly better 
on the training data compared to the test data, which did 
not happen in this case. This suggests that the model has 
been successfully regularized and is capable of 
generalizing well to new, unseen images. 
 
4 Performance Discussion 
The high accuracy (95-96%) in both training and 
testing sets demonstrates that the proposed model is 
highly effective for image retrieval tasks. A few factors 
contribute to this impressive performance: 
1. Effective Feature Extraction: The combination of 
traditional image processing techniques, such as HOG 
and SIFT, with color-based features ensured that the 
model captured a comprehensive set of characteristics 
for each image. These features allowed the model to 
make more informed predictions when classifying 
images. 
2. SVM Classification: The Support Vector Machine 
classifier is known for its ability to perform well even 
with small or complex datasets. Its ability to find optimal 
hyperplanes, especially when using a kernel like RBF, 
contributed significantly to the model’s accuracy. 
3. Regularization and Hyperparameter Tuning: The 
use of regularization techniques and hyperparameter 
optimization, such as selecting the appropriate kernel 
and C (regularization parameter), helped prevent 
overfitting and allowed the model to generalize well to 
the testing data.
